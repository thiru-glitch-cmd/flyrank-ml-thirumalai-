Can ML Beat a Hand-Written Rule for Content Refresh Prioritization?

A Random Forest model trained on 30,000 pseudonymized pages achieves a Precision@50 of 0.680 — a 2.8× lift over a production-style rule baseline — and outputs an explainable ranked refresh queue that helps editors spend their hours on the pages that actually need attention.

Abstract

Organic search content naturally decays as search intent shifts and competitors update their pages — yet editorial teams face thousands of articles with no reliable way to know which ones to refresh first. Using a curated, public-safe dataset of 30,000 pseudonymized pages across 32 clients from the FlyRank Search Intelligence platform (90-day GSC + GA4 performance windows), we train a binary classifier to predict whether a page's impression volume will decline in the most recent 30-day window. Evaluated on a strict client-holdout split to prevent information leakage, our Random Forest model achieves a Precision@50 of 0.680 — a 2.8× lift over a production-style hand-written rule baseline (Precision@50 = 0.240) and 0.747 ROC-AUC. The model's ranked output, combined with interpretable reason codes (e.g. declining_with_demand, ctr_review_candidate), directly supports an editor's decision on which pages to audit, rewrite, or monitor each week.

Section 01

Introduction & Problem Statement

A mid-sized digital publisher with 5,000 active articles cannot refresh everything. A content manager starts Monday with a blank audit list and a hard question: "which ten pages do I actually open today?" The usual answer is an opaque, manually maintained spreadsheet built on two or three rules — pages older than 180 days, or pages where impressions fell last month. That answer is too slow and too blunt.

💡 Core Decision: Given a large inventory of pages, rank the top 50 candidates for editorial review so that editors spend their limited hours on pages that are truly at risk of decline.

The business cost of getting this wrong is asymmetric. A false positive (flagging a healthy page) wastes editorial hours. A false negative (missing a decaying page) means a permanent traffic loss that compounds as the page falls further in rankings. This asymmetry motivates using Precision@50 as the primary metric: of the top-50 pages the model flags, how many are genuinely declining?

A plain threshold rule isn't enough here: content performance is driven by the interaction of position, CTR, content age, word count, scroll engagement, and impression consistency. None of these signals is individually decisive, but together they form a learnable pattern that a Random Forest can capture and a rule cannot.

Section 02

Data Sources & Preparation

Release used: data/raw/content_refresh_anonymized.csv — the FlyRank ML Internship starter dataset (30,000 rows × 44 columns). Each row is one pseudonymized content item (page) with 90-day aggregate metrics from Google Search Console (GSC) and Google Analytics 4 (GA4), covering 32 clients. All client names, domains, URLs, titles, and raw search queries have been hashed or removed before release.

📅 Date Windows
  • Metrics aggregated over a trailing 90-day window
  • Trend label derived from last-30d vs. prev-30d impression comparison
  • All pages have ≥ 90 days of content age in this slice
  • Every row has ≥ 1 GSC impression
🛡 Exclusions & Leakage Guards
  • trend_direction & trend_pct — source of the label; never features
  • content_id, client_id — pseudonym IDs; splits only, never features
  • provider_used, model_used — LLM generation metadata; not performance signals
  • Blind fillna(0) avoided; has_ flags added for systematic missing data

Key Gotchas Addressed

  • Rate columns are ×100 percentages — ctr = 0.76 means 0.76%, not 76%. Applies to ctr, engagement_rate, scroll_rate, ai_traffic_pct, trend_pct.
  • avg_position = 0 means "no position data" (1,205 rows), not rank zero — treated as missing.
  • scroll_rate and ai_traffic_pct can exceed 100 (different measurement systems per denominator) — included as-is with documentation.
  • Missingness follows content_type: ~100% of feedly article rows have no keyword data — imputed with has-flags, not blind zeros.

Section 03

Methodology

Label Definition

is_declining_label = 1 when trend_direction == "down", which is defined as a >20% drop in impressions comparing the most recent 30 days to the prior 30 days (impressions_last_30d vs. impressions_prev_30d). Base rate: 54.2% (16,262 of 30,000 rows).

⚠️ Label Trap: Because is_declining_label is derived directly from trend_direction and trend_pct, those two columns must never appear as model features. This is the leakage the Notebook 02 demonstration is built around.

Assumptions

We assume that a page's trailing 90-day search and engagement signals contain information predictive of whether its impression trajectory in the most recent 30 days is declining. We do not assume we can identify causal drivers of decline (e.g. a Google algorithm update vs. editorial neglect). The model is observational and correlation-based.

Validation Design — Client-Holdout Split

To simulate real-world generalization, we hold out entire clients from the test set. 20% of clients (by client_id) are reserved as the test set; all their pages are withheld from training. This means the model is evaluated on page profiles it has never seen any representative pages from — a harder and more honest test than a random row split.

🚀 Train Set

27,675 rows from 80% of clients. Features: 18 numeric + 8 categorical (one-hot encoded). Categorical imputed to "unknown" rather than dropped.

🔒 Test Set (Held Out)

2,325 rows from the 20% held-out clients. Evaluated once, final — no hyperparameter tuning on test results.

Baseline Model

Before training any ML model, we build a transparent rule-based score designed to match what a production SEO dashboard would apply. The rule is designed to be honestly beatable — it contains real logic, not random noise: 

Baseline Score =
  0.40 × Visibility    (normalized impressions_90d)
+ 0.30 × Freshness Risk (normalized days_since_last_update)
+ 0.25 × Position Opp  (1 − normalized avg_position)
+ 0.05 × Depth Gap     (normalized word_count)

This represents the kind of heuristic a non-technical content team would actually use. Its Precision@50 of 0.240 is the floor our model must clear.

Models Evaluated

LR

Logistic Regression with standard scaling and balanced class weights. Interpretable upper bound on linear separability.

DT

Decision Tree (max depth 5, min samples leaf 50). Human-readable decision paths; natural baseline for rule-based comparison.

RF

Random Forest (200 trees, max depth 10, min samples leaf 25, balanced weights). Ensemble approach captures non-linear interactions.

Leakage Checks Run

  • Confirmed trend_direction and trend_pct absent from feature lists (validated in Notebook 02 — including via a deliberate injection to show the AUC jumps to near-1.0).
  • Grain-probed the feature vector to ensure no client's rows appear in both train and test.
  • Verified that aggregated 90-day metrics are available before the label period: the last-30d / prev-30d window is the label, and all 90-day totals are entirely historical from the moment of snapshot.

Section 05

Feature Interpretation

Feature importance from a Random Forest is not a causal claim — it measures which signals the model uses to split decisions, not which ones cause decline. With that framing, the top signals tell a coherent story:

Top reason codes attached to pages in the refresh queue. declining_with_demand appears on ~13,200 pages — these are pages with real search volume that are actively losing impressions.
📈 Top Predictors of Decline
  • days_with_impressions (16.1%) — Inconsistent visibility is the strongest early-warning sign. Pages shown on fewer than ~60 of 90 days are structurally fragile.
  • log_impressions_90d (12.8%) — Scale amplifies decline: high-volume pages losing ground are more detectably declining.
  • avg_position (10.8%) — Pages ranked 11–50 ("page 2 zone") are most vulnerable; top-3 pages have natural protection.
  • content_age_days (9.5%) — Older content decays faster without refreshes.
🔍 Secondary Signals
  • word_count / char_count (4.1% / 4.0%) — Thin content has less ranking resilience.
  • ctr (3.3%) — Low CTR at a given position is a signal of title/meta mismatch that precedes position drops.
  • scroll_rate (3.1%) — Poor engagement indicates intent mismatch that may feed back into ranking signals.
  • days_with_sessions (3.0%) — Similar to impression consistency: sparse traffic is fragile traffic.
Section 06

Limitations & Honest Framing

⚠️ This work is observational and directional. It supports editorial decisions; it does not prove causation and should not be used as an automatic publishing trigger.
  • 📊 Observed, not causal. The model identifies associations between content signals and impression trends. It cannot distinguish between decline caused by algorithm updates, seasonality, competitor changes, or editorial neglect. All results should be described as "associated with" or "directionally predictive of" decline.
  • 🔁 Cross-sectional snapshot. The 30k-row starter dataset is a single 90-day snapshot. It does not model trajectory — a page that was declining for 6 months and then stabilized looks the same as a page that has always been stable. The warehouse release (DuckDB over ~79M daily rows) allows time-series feature engineering that this snapshot cannot.
  • ⚖️ Label base rate is high. 54.2% of pages are labeled as declining, which means the task is nearly balanced but in a real production system the declining fraction may differ. Precision@50 is the honest metric here — not overall accuracy, which would flatter any majority-class predictor.
  • 🏢 Client panel is unbalanced. 32 clients contribute unequal rows. The client-holdout split is the right design response, but results may not generalize equally to clients with very different content volumes or industries.
  • 🔍 No query-level signal. This analysis uses aggregate page-level metrics. The full warehouse's fact_content_query_90d table (query diversity, rare-query share) is not used here — it would require careful leakage alignment and adds complexity beyond this starter-dataset scope.
  • 🚫 Not a Google-algorithm claim. Nothing in this analysis proves, infers, or implies knowledge of Google's proprietary ranking algorithm. Trends are measured in public search console data and are entirely observational.
Section 07

Ranked Action Recommendations

The model produces a ranked queue of 30,000 pages, each with a priority score, model probability, action tag, and reason codes. The recommended editorial workflow:

  1. Filter the queue for high-confidence pages (probability ≥ 0.65 → 3,576 pages).
  2. Sort by priority score descending to find the week's top 50.
  3. Read the reason codes to decide the action — the codes tell you why the page ranked here.
  4. Manually verify: is the decline real? Is it seasonal? Is the intent still correct?

Reproducibility

The full pipeline runs in under 2 minutes on a standard laptop from a fresh clone. Random seed 42 is set for all splits and model training.

cd flyrank-ml-internship-starter
pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/run_all.py
